01 • Educational background

Start with the common idea

Correlation is a family of ways to quantify how strongly data or signals are related. The exact meaning depends on what is being compared and whether time/lag is involved.

SIGNAL VS ITSELF

Autocorrelation

Autocorrelation compares a signal with a delayed copy of the same signal.

Rₓₓ[k] = Σ x[n] · x[n−k]

The result is a function of lag k. Repeated peaks can reveal periodic structure.

Example: estimating the pitch/periodicity of an audio waveform.
SIGNAL VS ANOTHER

Cross-correlation

Cross-correlation compares one signal with a shifted version of another signal.

Rₓᵧ[k] = Σ x[n] · y[n−k]

A strong peak can indicate the relative alignment or delay between the signals.

Example: comparing a transmitted radar pulse with its received echo.
Important: In signal processing, autocorrelation and cross-correlation are both functions of lag. “Correlation” in statistics often refers to a normalized, same-time linear relationship instead.

04 • The core difference

Difference Between Autocorrelation and Cross-Correlation

The central difference is what is being compared and what information the correlation peak reveals.

AUTOCORRELATION

Rₓₓ[k]

SAME SIGNAL → SHIFT → COMPARE
Signals used

One signal only.

Comparison

The signal is compared with a delayed version of itself.

Main purpose

Find repetition, periodicity and self-similarity.

Peak meaning

Zero-lag gives the strongest self-match; repeated peaks reveal periodic structure.

Typical applications

Pitch detection, vibration analysis and repeating-pattern detection.

CROSS-CORRELATION

Rₓᵧ[k]

SIGNAL X → SHIFT SIGNAL Y → COMPARE
Signals used

Two different signals.

Comparison

One signal is compared with a shifted version of another signal.

Main purpose

Find similarity and relative time delay.

Peak meaning

The peak position shows the lag where the two signals align best.

Typical applications

Radar, synchronization, signal alignment and pattern matching.

05 • How to read the plots

Interpret the correlation curve

Do not just look for a line. Ask what the peak, its position and its width are telling you.

⬆

Peak height

A higher normalized peak means a stronger match at that lag.

↔

Peak position

The lag where the maximum occurs indicates the relative alignment or delay.

⌁

Peak width

A narrow peak often indicates a precise timing match; a broad peak means many nearby lags give similar matches.

↻

Repeated peaks

Regularly spaced peaks are a visual clue that the signal contains periodic structure.

Presentation tip: Move the lag sliders during your explanation. First show autocorrelation at zero lag, then move away from zero. Next introduce a delayed second signal and show how cross-correlation finds the alignment.
Final takeaway

Remember just two words

AUTO = SAME signal → discover repetition.
CROSS = DIFFERENT signals → discover alignment / delay.
